1 · The mission

What we're doing, in one minute

Some employees misuse access they legitimately have: they read records they have no business reading, or copy files on their way out the door. Their logins are valid, so passwords and firewalls don't stop them. We catch them by watching behavior.

Input

Activity logs

Who logged into which computer and when, USB drives plugged in, files copied, websites visited, plus the org chart.

Engine

Several detectors

Each detector looks for one kind of strange behavior. We also tested a recommender system, the kind Netflix uses; section 7 shows what it did and didn't add.

Output

A short review list

One ranked list of people to check, with the reasons behind each score, an experimental view for out-of-role logins, and an AI-written explanation an analyst can verify.

2 · Problem employees

What is a "problematic employee", and how do we know?

Security teams call them insider threats: people inside the organization who use legitimate access to cause harm, on purpose (stealing, leaking, sabotage) or by snooping where they shouldn't.

How do we know who is a real insider? In real companies you rarely do. Confirmed cases come from investigations, HR complaints or police, months later, so there are almost no labels to train on. That's why most of our system works without labels. For testing, we use a benchmark where the answer is known: CMU's CERT dataset [1], a simulated company where a "red team" planted insiders and recorded exactly who they are.

The four kinds of insider in our data

Scenario 1 · Leaker

Never worked late or used USB drives. Suddenly logs in after hours, plugs in a USB drive, uploads data to wikileaks.org, then quits.

30 in r4.2, 29 in r5.2. Sudden spike.

Scenario 2 · Job-hunter thief

Browses job sites and competitors, then copies files to USB far more than usual over about two months, then quits.

30 in each release. Slow drift; the hardest.

Scenario 3 · Angry sysadmin

Downloads a keylogger, plants it on the supervisor's PC by USB, logs in as the supervisor, sends a panic email, quits.

10 in each release. Sudden spike.

Scenario 4 · Snooper

Logs into other people's computers, searches their files and emails them home, more often over three months. Never leaves.

30 in r5.2 only. Out-of-role access.

Why we never use "left the company"a trap

In r4.2, all 70 insiders leave the company, and only 155 of 1,000 employees leave in total. A detector that just asked "did they quit?" would look brilliant. But you only know someone quit after the damage is done, so it's hindsight, not detection. Using information from the future is called leakage, and judges who know this dataset will check for it.

3 · How it's done today

The regular ways of catching insiders

Companies mostly use four tools. Each catches something; each leaves a gap our project targets.

ApproachHow it worksGood atGap
Fixed rules (data-loss prevention)"Alert if someone copies over 50 files to USB" or "after-hours USB use".Simple, explainableSame threshold for everyone; floods analysts; misses anything slow. Our hand rule found only 6 insiders in its top 20.
Access control and reviewsManagers approve who may open what; periodic access reviews.Prevents obvious misuseInsiders use access they already have. It can't see how access is used.
Behavior baselines (UEBA products)Compare each person with their own history and their peers; score unusual days.Sudden changesWeak on slow drift and on "touched the wrong thing" when volume looks normal.
Supervised machine learningTrain a classifier on past confirmed cases.Repeats of known attacksNeeds many labeled cases (rare), and it memorizes known attacks. We measured this (section 13).

Our project starts from the third row, which is the honest baseline, and adds what it misses: each person's history expressed as weekly percentiles, weekly drift detectors for slow attacks, and a persistence signal for repeated out-of-role logins, combined without labels. We also tested a recommender, and report honestly how little it added.

How commercial products do it (and what we borrowed)industry
  • Microsoft Sentinel UEBA [14] compares each user to their 20 closest peers and shows plain flags like "first time user performed action" or "uncommon among peers".
  • Microsoft Purview Insider Risk [15] only spotlights an alert with a high score and several corroborating conditions; users are pseudonymized by default.
  • Splunk and Exabeam [16][17] keep separate detections that each add risk to a person, with reasons listed for the analyst.

We tested that idea against a single fused ranking at the same review budget. Separate lists won only when one attack type needed its own list (section 12), so our default is a fused ranking with a corroboration flag, plus a separate view for out-of-role logins.

What each tool does (open any one)

IBM LinuxONE and the Telum chiprequired by the event

IBM's enterprise server, the kind banks and hospitals run their core records on. Its processor type is s390x (laptops are x86 or ARM). The Telum processor has an AI accelerator built onto the chip. Why it matters to us: detection and the AI explanation run right next to the sensitive data, so logs never leave the machine. That is the theme of our track, "Innovation without exposure". Setup and accelerator details: [22][23].

Docker and Jupyterwhere we work

Docker packages a complete software setup (Python, libraries, notebook server) into a container that runs the same anywhere. The event hands us one. Jupyter is a notebook in the browser: write a few lines of Python, run them, see the result underneath. We build, test and demo there.

Apache Spark (PySpark)big data

Spark processes data too big for one computer's memory by splitting it into chunks and working on them in parallel. PySpark is its Python interface. "Local mode" uses all the cores of a single machine, so no cluster is needed. The 14.5 GB web log would crash a normal pandas script, which loads the whole file into memory; Spark reads it in pieces and keeps only the columns we ask for. On LinuxONE we install Java 17 and PySpark ourselves, and avoid pandas UDFs because the Arrow library doesn't exist for s390x.

Spark MLlib ALS (the recommender)section 7

ALS ("alternating least squares") is the recommender algorithm from our Big Data course lab. It learns a hidden "taste profile" for every person and every item from who used what [24]. We used it in reverse: an access the recommender would never suggest for you is surprising. Section 7 shows how much that actually helped.

pandas and scikit-learneveryday ML

pandas is Python's table library; once Spark shrinks the data, pandas handles the small tables. scikit-learn is the standard machine-learning library: it gives us Isolation Forest, LOF, logistic regression and the scoring metrics.

Isolation Forest, LOF, autoencoderanomaly detectors
  • Isolation Forest makes random cuts through the data; odd points get separated in very few cuts.
  • LOF (Local Outlier Factor) asks whether a point sits in a much emptier neighborhood than its neighbors do. On weekly data it found 12 to 13 of the 30 job-hunters, which almost nothing else found.
  • Autoencoder: a small neural network trained to compress and rebuild normal weeks. It rebuilds unusual weeks badly, and the error is the score. It can run on the Telum chip through IBM's TensorFlow or PyTorch plugins.
Stouffer fusionhow signals combine

Each detector's ranking becomes a p-value (how rare it is to score this high). Within each family of similar detectors we keep the strongest, then add the families' evidence on a z-scale (Stouffer's method) [19]. Grouping first stops near-duplicate detectors from outvoting the rest. It uses no labels.

IBM Granite with llama.cppthe explanation

Granite is IBM's open large language model; llama.cpp runs it on the server's own CPU, with no internet AI service. We give it only an evidence pack (facts with IDs, like "E2: 6 USB connects this week, usual is 0") and require it to cite them. Code then checks that every number it wrote exists in the pack [20]. Log text is treated as data, never instructions [21], and names are replaced with IDs. Running Granite on s390x: [26].

6 · The baseline

Why we need a baseline, and what it already catches

A baseline is the simplest reasonable method. Anything fancier only counts if it beats it. Weather forecasters know this: "tomorrow will be like today" is surprisingly hard to beat.

Hand rule

"Count the days someone used USB after hours."

top 20: 6 insiders AUC 0.77

Personal baseline

"How unusual is today compared with your own normal?" for each count, over each day and over rolling 28 days.

top 20: 20 insiders AUC 0.96

The baseline is strong. On r4.2 it catches all 30 leakers and nearly all sysadmins: the "sudden spike" attacks. Every addition after this has to earn its place against it.
Its blind spot: only 2 to 5 of the 30 job-hunters reach its top 100. They change slowly, and their volume looks normal. Snoopers (r5.2) are invisible too: logging into someone else's computer adds one login, not a spike.
The math of "unusual for you", in plain wordsz-score

For every person and every count (say USB connects), take their average and spread during the training months. A test day's score is how many "spreads" above their average it sits. Example: someone averages 0.2 USB connects a day with a spread of 0.5. A day with 6 connects scores (6 - 0.2) / (0.5 + 1) = 3.9. We add 1 to the spread so people with zero history don't get infinite scores. Each person's final score is their worst day.

The 28-day version compares a rolling four-week total with what the person's usual daily rate predicts, which helps a little with slow ramps.

7 · The recommender

The recommender: what it is, and what it really added

Netflix learns your taste from what you watch and recommends what people like you watched. We learned each employee's "taste" in websites and computers, then flipped it: an access the recommender would never suggest for you is suspicious. We tested it carefully. The honest answer: it adds a little, not a lot.

What "out-of-profile destination" means. A destination is a website (or a computer). Your profile is the set of places people with habits like yours visit. A finance analyst on wikileaks.org, or an IT admin on keylogger sites, is far outside that profile, so the recommender flags it.

How much did it contribute? Three measurements

1 · its own job

Predicting normal browsing

Before asking whether it catches insiders, we asked whether it learns anything: can it predict which new sites a normal employee will visit? Its average rank for the sites people actually visited next was 35.9%, against 35.5% for simply guessing the most popular sites (50% is random). On the harder dataset: 35.8% vs 38.3%. In this simulated company, "taste" barely exists beyond popularity.

2 · as one ingredient

Inside a combined model

Added to the other signals, it helps a little: +4 insiders in the top 70 over a naive "new site" check in an early test (41 to 45), +1 once our best signals are in (51 to 52), and on the harder dataset 45 to 49 when added on top of naive novelty.

3 · as its own review list

A dedicated queue

Given its own share of a 100-person review budget, it found 5 insiders the other lists missed but pushed out 11 others: 53 found with it, 59 without on the harder dataset. On r4.2: 42 with it, 41 without, and 44 with a plain rare-site list instead.

Why not RMSE?your question

RMSE (root mean squared error) measures how far predicted numbers are from true numbers. Catching insiders is a ranking task: there is no number to predict, only an order of people. So for detection we compare rankings with and without the recommender (measurements 2 and 3).

For the recommender's own job we did compute RMSE on a visit/no-visit grid. It came out worse than popularity (0.539 vs 0.426 on r4.2), because implicit-feedback scores are preferences, not probabilities. That is why implicit recommenders are judged by ranking, the percentile rank in measurement 1 [13].

The colleague's-computer story, corrected

what we first thought

On r5.2 the recommender caught only 7 of 30 snoopers, people who log into colleagues' computers, while an ownership rule caught 30. We concluded that collaborative filtering forgives a colleague's computer because the colleague is similar.

what the controlled test showed

Our reviewer asked us to count all three the same way: weeks of logins to unusual computers, discounted for people who already roamed. Ownership, naive and recommender versions each caught 27 of 30. The real difference was how we counted (persistence over weeks), not the scoring idea. Ownership only sharpens the top of the list (17 vs 14 insiders in the top 20).

Our verdict. We keep the recommender as an optional piece of evidence ("nobody in your peer group visits this site") inside the fused ranking. We present it honestly as an idea we tested that added less than we hoped. The evidence trail that changed our design is part of our pitch.
How ALS actually learns a "taste"for the curious

Picture the person-by-site table above with millions of cells. ALS finds, for every person, a short list of 16 numbers (their taste vector), and for every site 16 numbers (its profile), so that a person's vector times a site's profile approximates how much they use it. It alternates: fix the sites and solve for people, then fix the people and solve for sites, until it settles. That's the "alternating least squares".

"Implicit feedback" means we have no ratings, only usage counts, so heavier use counts as more confidence [13]. Our settings: 16 taste numbers, confidence weight 10, regularization 0.1, trained only on the training months.

Peer-of-item, the variant we kept: for a new site, find the most similar previous visitor [12][11]. It is a strong ranking signal on its own (AUC 0.84 on r4.2; on r5.2 it puts all 29 leakers and all 10 sysadmins in its top 100), largely because many attack sites had no previous visitors at all.

Prior workfor the pitch

Scoring access by how unexpected it is under a collaborative-filtering model has been used against hospital-record snooping (Menon and colleagues, 2014) [9], and Google's insider-detection system Facade [11] builds on matrix factorization of who-accessed-what. Our contribution is a controlled measurement of how much, and how little, it adds on a public benchmark.

8 · More signals

The signals that carry the system

Different attacks leave different footprints, so we use a small team of detector families. Each is good at one kind of attack and blind to others; the fused ranking combines them.

sudden spike

Own-history percentile

Each week, for each count: what share of your own past weeks were lower? A week where several counts beat everything you did before is a spike [5]. The best top-of-list signal on both datasets: its top 20 were all insiders (39.4 of 70 and 40.3 of 99 in the top 70).

slow drift

Weekly LOF, unusual weeks

Weekly LOF found 12 to 13 of 30 job-hunters on r4.2 and 11 on r5.2, the hardest attack type. Counting how many weeks were unusual found 19.5 of 30 snoopers on r5.2.

out-of-role (experimental)

Persistent logins to unusual computers

The weeks someone logs into computers they didn't use before, discounted for people whose job already involved roaming. 27 to 30 of 30 snoopers in the top 100 on r5.2. Designed after seeing r5.2, so it is shown as an experimental view.

unusual destination

Rare sites + recommender

Visits to sites under 1% of staff visited: 41 of 70 on r4.2. Recommender peer-of-item: 31 of 99 on r5.2. Each wins on one dataset; both catch leakers and the sysadmins.

Also tried: peers, more windows, other modelssupporting signals
  • Team peer comparison (the reviewer's suggestion for "covering a colleague's duties"): useful but moderate alone (14 of its top 20 were insiders).
  • 7- and 56-day windows, worst-3-days averages, Isolation Forest: each strong alone, mostly overlapping with the baseline.
  • Popularity-corrected recommender and a simple 28-day site-visit lift: did not help, so they were dropped.

Which attacks each detector catches
Share of each scenario's insiders in the detector's top 100

What the metrics meanP@20, AP, AUC
  • P@20: of the top 20 people flagged, the share who are real insiders. What an analyst feels on day one.
  • Found in top 70: how many insiders appear in the first 70 names (r4.2 has 70 insiders; r5.2 has 99). When scores tie, we report the expected count, so a few numbers are fractional.
  • AP (average precision): one number for the whole ranking that rewards putting insiders near the top. 1.0 is perfect.
  • AUC: the chance a random insider ranks above a random normal employee. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect.
  • Top 100 by scenario: how many insiders of one type make the first 100 names, which shows what a detector catches.
12 · Combining

How we combine detectors, and which way won

We compared three ways of combining detectors at the same review budget. The answer depends on the mix of threats, so we chose the one that beat the baseline on both datasets.

Adding signals to a trained combiner (r4.2)
Insiders found in the top 70, out of 70

Each added signal helps a little. But this combiner is trained on labels, and section 13 shows it breaks on attacks it never saw.

way 1

Trained combiner

Learns how much to trust each detector from labeled past cases. Best in testing (63 and 55 insiders among 100 reviewed), but it needs labels, and with job-hunters hidden from training it caught only 4 to 7 of 30.

way 2 · our default

Fused ranking, no labels

Family-first Stouffer fusion, chosen on r4.2 and frozen before the second test. It beat the baseline on both datasets: 55 vs 44 and 43 vs 39 insiders among 100 reviewed.

way 3

Specialist queues

Each detector fills its own review list. Won on r5.2 (53 vs 43) because snoopers needed their own list; lost on r4.2 (42 vs 55). We keep the idea only as a separate out-of-role view.

Insiders found among exactly 100 people reviewed
The decision: the fused ranking is the default list, because it beat the simple baseline on both datasets with no labels. Out-of-role logins get their own experimental view, because blending hides them (see below). The trained combiner is shown only as a reference that needs labels.
try it

Queue simulator: 12 simulated employees

This shows when separate lists beat a blended score: when an attacker scores high on only one signal. Each employee has a score from 0 to 100 on four detectors. Specialist queues take the top scorers of each detector; a blended score averages the four and reviews the same number of people. Pick the slots per queue, then reveal who the insiders are.

Slots per queue

All 12 people and their scores are invented for teaching. On real data, this situation happened on r5.2 (snoopers): queues found 53 vs the fused ranking's 43 among 100 reviewed. On r4.2, with no such attack type, the fused ranking won 55 vs 42.

Why blending hides specialiststhe key lesson

When you combine several detectors, a person flagged strongly by only one of them gets pulled back toward normal by the others. That's fine for spiky leakers, whom everyone flags, but costly for snoopers, whom only the out-of-role signal sees. Folded into the fused score, it kept only 4 to 8 of 30 snoopers in the top 100; as its own list, 20 of its 25 slots were snoopers. That's why out-of-role logins get their own view.

13 · Can we trust it?

The tests that keep us honest

It's easy to fool yourself with machine learning. We ran four checks, and each one changed the design.

Hide the job-hunters from training: how many does each method still catch?
r4.2, job-hunters in the top 100, out of 30
test 1 · unseen attack

Gradient boosting scored 60 of 70, too good to be true. With job-hunters hidden from training it caught 4 of 30. A label-free sum caught 16. Generalization drops sharply for attack types a model never saw.

test 2 · a second dataset

We froze the pipeline and ran it unchanged on r5.2. The fused ranking and own-history percentiles held; the queue design and the recommender story did not.

test 3 · no peeking

Training ends before the first attack, the week containing each cutoff is dropped, and "left the company" is never used. Some choices were still made with r4.2's answers in view, which is why r5.2 was the real test.

test 4 · outside review

Another AI model (Codex) reviewed the full study three times. It found 4 real bugs: an unbalanced hold-out test, a leaked training week, tie luck from user-ID order, and a silently swapped comparison. All were fixed and re-run, overturning 3 headline claims.

What we still can't claimsay it in the pitch
  • Retrospective, not real-time: each person is scored over the whole test period; how quickly we'd catch someone isn't measured.
  • Job-hunters stay hard: 0 to 16 of 30 in the top 100, depending on the method. Published research reports 12.5 to 74% [6].
  • The data is simulated. Labeled real insider logs are very rare; LANL releases real authentication logs with red-team labels, a good third test.
  • The out-of-role view and the build plan were shaped after seeing r5.2, so they need an untouched test.
