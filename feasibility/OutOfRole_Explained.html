1 · The mission

What we're doing, in one minute

Some employees misuse access they legitimately have: they read records they have no business reading, or copy files on their way out the door. Their logins are valid, so passwords and firewalls don't stop them. We catch them by watching behavior while they are still active, and we suggest which access to pre-approve so that everything else gets a reviewer's look.

Part 1 · the headline

Early warning

Replay the logs one day at a time. Compare each person with their own normal weeks; the 20 most unusual people form that day's review list.

Part 2

Granite case files

For each person on the list, code picks the evidence ("new to this person, rarest first"). IBM's Granite model picks a summary label and the facts that support it; code writes every sentence of the case file, and any other reply is discarded.

Part 3

Least-privilege advisor

A recommender predicts which websites each person is likely to use next, so those can be pre-approved. Anything else goes to a reviewer; sites nobody in the company has visited are blocked.

Input: activity logs from one company (logons, USB drives, file copies, websites). Output: a short daily review list, a checked case file for each person on it, and suggested pre-approvals for each person. Parts 1 and 2 never use the recommender; part 3 is where it earns its place.

2 · Problem employees

What is a "problematic employee", and how do we know?

Security teams call them insider threats: people inside the organization who use legitimate access to cause harm, on purpose (stealing, leaking, sabotage) or by snooping where they shouldn't.

How do we know who is a real insider? In real companies you rarely do. Confirmed cases come from investigations, HR complaints or police, months later, so there are almost no labels to train on. That's why our system works without labels. For testing, we use a benchmark where the answer is known: CMU's CERT dataset [1], a simulated company where a "red team" planted insiders and recorded exactly who they are.

The four kinds of insider in our data

Scenario 1 · Leaker

Never worked late or used USB drives. Suddenly logs in after hours, plugs in a USB drive, uploads data to wikileaks.org, then quits.

30 in r4.2, 29 in r5.2. Sudden spike. Daily list: 25 of 30 and 26 of 29 in time; median delay 0 days from the first labelled malicious day, mostly because they were already on the list (only 7 of 30 and 15 of 29 newly flagged).

Scenario 2 · Job-hunter thief

Browses job sites and competitors, then copies files to USB far more than usual over about two months, then quits.

30 in each release. Slow drift. Daily list: 30 of 30 and 27 of 30 in time, median delay 11 and 21.5 days.

Scenario 3 · Angry sysadmin

Downloads a keylogger, plants it on the supervisor's PC by USB, logs in as the supervisor, sends a panic email, quits.

10 in each release. Over in about two days. Daily list: only 4 of 10 and 2 of 10 in time.

Scenario 4 · Snooper

Logs into other people's computers, searches their files and emails them home, more often over three months. Never leaves.

30 in r5.2 only. Out-of-role access. Daily list: 25 of 30 in time, median delay 21.5 days.

Why we never use "left the company"a trap

In r4.2, every insider leaves the company, while most employees never do. A detector that just asked "did they quit?" would look brilliant. But you only know someone quit after the damage is done, so it's hindsight, not detection. Using information from the future is called leakage, and judges who know this dataset will check for it. Our tests never read departures.

3 · How it's done today

The regular ways of catching insiders

Companies mostly use four tools. Each catches something; each leaves a gap our project targets.

ApproachHow it worksGood atGap
Fixed rules (data-loss prevention)"Alert if someone copies over 50 files to USB" or "after-hours USB use".Simple, explainableThe same threshold for everyone; floods analysts with busy people; misses anything slow.
Access control and reviewsManagers approve who may open what; periodic access reviews.Prevents obvious misuseGrants pile up, and insiders use access they already have. Reviews can't see how access is used.
Behavior baselines (UEBA products)Compare each person with their own history and their peers; score unusual days.Sudden changesUsually judged after the fact; weak on slow drift and on "touched the wrong thing" when volume looks normal.
Supervised machine learningTrain a classifier on past confirmed cases.Repeats of known attacksNeeds many labeled cases (rare), and it memorizes known attacks. We measured this (step 7).

Our project starts from the third row, the honest baseline, and adds three things: a day-by-day early-warning list measured on whether it flags people in time, case files whose every fact is written by code from the evidence (Granite only picks a label and fact IDs), and a least-privilege advisor that uses a recommender to suggest which access to pre-approve.

How commercial products do it (and what we borrowed)industry
  • Microsoft Sentinel UEBA [14] compares each user to their closest peers and shows plain flags like "first time user performed action" or "uncommon among peers". Our evidence rule starts with "new to this person" for the same reason.
  • Microsoft Purview Insider Risk [15] pseudonymizes users by default. So do we (U-017).
  • Splunk and Exabeam [16][17] keep separate detections that each add risk to a person, with reasons listed for the analyst.

We tested separate queues against one combined ranking at the same review budget in the earlier study: queues won only on r5.2, where snoopers needed a list of their own (step 14). The build uses one daily list, and every name on it gets a case file with its reasons.

What each tool does (open any one)

IBM LinuxONE and the Telum chiprequired by the event

IBM's enterprise server, the kind banks and hospitals run their core records on. Its processor type is s390x (laptops are x86 or ARM). The Telum processor has an AI accelerator built onto the chip. Why it matters to us: detection, the recommender and the AI explanation all run right next to the sensitive data, so logs never leave the machine. That is the theme of our track, "Innovation without exposure". Setup and accelerator details: [22][23]. The demo video shows uname -m printing s390x as proof.

Docker and Jupyterwhere we work

Docker packages a complete software setup (Python, libraries, notebook server) into a container that runs the same anywhere. The event hands us one, and our final container plus a GitHub repo with a README are part of the submission (role R1 in the build plan). Jupyter is a notebook in the browser: write a few lines of Python, run them, see the result underneath.

Apache Spark (PySpark)big data

Spark processes data too big for one computer's memory by splitting it into chunks and working on them in parallel. PySpark is its Python interface. "Local mode" uses all the cores of a single machine, so no cluster is needed. The 14.5 GB web log would crash a normal pandas script, which loads the whole file into memory; Spark reads it in pieces and keeps only the columns we ask for. On LinuxONE we install Java 17 and PySpark ourselves, and avoid pandas UDFs because pyarrow doesn't exist for s390x.

ALS, the recommendersection 9

ALS ("alternating least squares") is the recommender algorithm from our Big Data course lab [24]. It learns a hidden "taste profile" for every person and every site from who visited what. Our first idea used it as a detector, and it was no better than popularity there. Tuned without labels, it now predicts each person's next new websites, so the least-privilege advisor can suggest them for pre-approval. The build uses a small numpy version (recommender_als_simple.py); Spark's ALS scales regularization differently, so if we switch to it we re-tune on the last training month.

numpy, pandas and scikit-learneveryday ML

pandas handles tables once Spark shrinks the data; numpy runs the daily replay (early_warning_simple.py is 49 lines) and the ALS model. scikit-learn (Isolation Forest, LOF, logistic regression) was used in the earlier study and is not needed for the build.

IBM Granite with llama.cppthe explanation

Granite is IBM's open large language model; llama.cpp runs it on the server's own CPU, with no internet AI service. On s390x the model file must be big-endian [26]. We give Granite only an evidence pack (facts with IDs, E1, E2 and so on), and it replies with JSON only: a label from a fixed list and the IDs of the facts that support it. It writes no free text; code writes every fact sentence from the pack and discards any other reply [20]. Log text is treated as data, never instructions [21], and names are replaced with pseudonyms. Section 8 has a working example.

6 · The baseline

Why we need a baseline, and what it already catches

A baseline is the simplest reasonable method. Anything fancier only counts if it beats it. Weather forecasters know this: "tomorrow will be like today" is surprisingly hard to beat.

Hand rule

"Count the days someone used USB after hours." The same threshold for everyone, so busy people flood the list and slow attacks slip under it. It was a reference point in the earlier study [0], never a contender.

Personal baseline, 28 days

"How unusual are your last 28 days compared with what your own daily rate predicts?" Ranked after the fact, it put 38 insiders in its top 70 on r4.2 (36 on r5.2) with P@20 1.00 and 0.90.

Test: v3 study, step 3

Our results call this baseline R2. It is not the same thing as role R2 (Detection) in the build plan.

The baseline is strong. At 100 reviews it finds 44 insiders on r4.2 and 39 on r5.2, where 100 random people would hold 7 and 4.95. Replayed day by day and scored on each day's value alone, on the same 20-name list as ours, it flags 59 of 70 (r4.2) and 40 of 99 (r5.2) in time: a tie on r4.2, half of ours on r5.2. Under our running rule it gets only 33 and 28. Tests: v3 study (L1 above); early_warning_full.py.
Its blind spot: only 5 of the 30 job-hunters reached its top 100 (step 8). They change slowly, and their volume looks normal. Snoopers (r5.2) are hard too: one login to a colleague's PC adds one login, not a spike.
The math of "unusual for you", in plain wordsz-score

For every person and every count (say USB connects), take their average and spread during the training months. A test day's score is how many "spreads" above their average it sits. Made-up example: someone averages 0.2 USB connects a day with a spread of 0.5. A day with 6 connects scores (6 - 0.2) / (0.5 + 1) = 3.9. We add 1 to the spread so people with zero history don't get infinite scores.

The 28-day version (R2) compares a rolling four-week total with what the person's usual daily rate predicts, which helps a little with slow ramps.

7 · Early warning

Early warning: a short list every morning, while insiders are still active

Most insider studies rank people after the fact, once the logs are complete. We replay the logs one day at a time, using only what was known that day, and ask each morning: who is acting most unlike their own normal right now? The 20 highest scores make that day's review list.

1 · your own normal

Compare with your own weeks

For each person, add up the last 7 days of nine counts: logons, USB connects, file copies and web visits (each also counted after hours), plus distinct computers used. A count is unusual when it beats every one of that person's training weeks.

2 · memory

Keep a running score

Each day adds the number of unusual counts to a running score that halves every 7 days. A burst fades within a couple of weeks; a steady drift keeps building.

3 · the list

Review the top 20

The 20 highest running scores are that day's list. About 5 first-time names a week reach the analyst (4.8 on r4.2, 5.3 on r5.2); about 30 distinct people pass through the list in a week (30.2, 32.6).

Who else lands on the listanalyst load

How "by chance" and the random control were measuredcontrols

Chance: take a random normal employee's actual days on the list and pretend they belonged to an insider. How often would that history count as in time? On average 0.038 of r4.2 insiders (about 3 of 70) and 0.029 of r5.2 insiders (about 3 of 99); even the 95th percentile is only 0.071 and 0.061.

Random score: give everyone a random score but keep the same 20-name list and the same decay rule, over 20 seeds. That flags 10.3 (r4.2) and 11.4 (r5.2) in time. It does better than chance because a random list churns about 4 times faster than the real one, so more people pass through it. The real list's 59 and 80 are far above both.

The fair baseline: under our running rule (a score that halves every 7 days) the 28-day baseline keeps the same few people on the list and flags only 33 and 28. Scored on each day's value alone, its daily list churn (9.1% and 8.9%) is close to ours (9.2% and 11.3%), and it flags 59 and 40. That is the comparison we lead with. Tests: early_warning_full.py, the independent validation and Codex review round 4.

Named in advance vs chosen afterhonesty

Before running the replay we pre-registered one comparison: a fusion of detector families against the 28-day baseline under the same running rule. It passed on both releases (McNemar p < 0.001), flagging 60 of 70 and 54 of 99 (54.5%) in time. The percentile list was chosen as the headline after seeing the results. On r4.2 the fusion was one insider ahead (60 vs 59); on r5.2 the percentile list was far ahead (80 vs 54). We say this in the pitch. Test: early_warning_full.py.

Caveatsread before presenting
  • 25 of 30 (r4.2) and 24 of 29 (r5.2) leakers show unlabelled after-hours USB activity in the week before their labelled start, so some were already on the list when their attack "began". Counting only insiders not listed in the 14 days before their first malicious action: 38 of 70 and 67 of 99.
  • Insiders make up 5 to 7% of staff in CERT, which is unrealistically high, and the data is synthetic.
  • It is a replay of past logs, not a real-time production system.
  • The independent validator found no look-ahead and no label leakage in the scoring code; a one-month-later cutoff also left the headline intact.
The math, in plain wordsfor the Detection role

For each day and count: what share of the person's training weeks were lower than the last 7 days (ties count half)? If the last 7 days beat essentially all of them, that count is unusual today. Today's score is the number of unusual counts plus a tiny tie-breaker from the percentiles. The running score is yesterday's running score times a factor that halves it over 7 days, plus today's score. Fixed settings, decided before r5.2 and not tuned afterwards: the beat-every-week rule, the 7-day half-life and the 20-name list (build plan).

8 · Granite case files

Granite case files: an explanation the analyst can check

A name on a list is not enough; the analyst needs to know why, fast. For each person on the list, code picks the evidence, and IBM's Granite model picks a summary label and the facts that support it. Granite writes no free text: code writes every sentence of the case file from the evidence pack.

1 · code

Evidence pack

Each person's activity becomes items: a computer logged into on a day, a site visited on a day, a USB day, an after-hours day. Items new to this person come first; within each group, the rarest across staff. The top items go into the pack with an ID, the person's own normal and dates, under a pseudonym. Optionally, two slots come from this rule and one from the recommender (see below).

2 · Granite

Picks a label and the facts

Granite replies with JSON only, with exactly two keys: label, one of "possible data theft", "possible data leak", "possible snooping", "unusual after-hours activity" or "weak evidence"; and facts, the IDs of the supporting facts (E1, E2 and so on). If evidence strength is weak, the label must be "weak evidence". Text inside the facts is data, never instructions [21].

3 · code

Reply check

Any other reply is discarded: an extra key such as a free-text note, an unknown fact ID, a label not on the list, non-JSON text or malformed values. The analyst then sees a deterministic fallback labelled "needs review" (or "weak evidence"), so the step fails closed. LLM explanations drift from the facts [20]; a reply with no free text has nothing to drift. Test: case_file.py.

4 · code

Case file and evidence strength

Code renders every fact sentence from the pack, always shows the top 3 ranked items and marks the ones Granite cited. Every rule always cites something, even for a normal employee, so each case file states how strong its evidence is. It is context for the analyst, never a reason to dismiss anyone (see below). Test: evidence_ranking.py.

A fact sentence as code writes it: 2010-08-02: visited website "wikileaks.org" (not observed in this person's training history; used by under 0.1% of staff during training) [E1]

Each one is rejected without a crash and replaced by the fallback; weak and empty evidence packs fall back too. Test: case_file.py.

    9 · The recommender
    
  
    The recommender: a poor detector, a good access predictor
    
  
    Netflix learns your taste from what you watch and recommends what people like you watched. We learned each employee's "taste" in websites. Our first idea flipped it into a detector: an access the recommender would never suggest is suspicious. That did not work. What does work, once tuned, is its original job: predicting which new websites a person is likely to use next, so the advisor can suggest them for pre-approval.
    
  
    The pivot, stated plainly. Our abstract promised recommender-based detection. Tests showed that own-history baselines detect better, so the recommender moved to access advice. Detection now never depends on it.
    
  
    Superseded claim. The earlier study concluded that the recommender did no better than popularity even at predicting normal browsing (mean percentile rank 35.9 vs 35.5, lower is better; step 15). That came from untuned settings: rank 16, alpha 10, regularization 0.1. Re-tuned without labels (rank 64, alpha 0.5, regularization 1.0 on log(1 + active days)), its top 20 catches 0.211 of each normal employee's next new websites against 0.145 for popularity on r4.2, and 0.190 against 0.130 on r5.2. Tests: recommender_als_simple.py, least_privilege_full.py.

    What it is not
    
  
    
    
    not a detector
    As a detector, its site-surprise score put 5 insiders in its top 70 against 38 for the baseline (step 4).     At flag time, recommender surprise beats C1 on r4.2 (0.75 to 0.77 against 0.66 in the validator's evidence window, thanks to job-hunters) but loses clearly on r5.2 (0.46 tuned, 0.70 untuned, against 0.85).     not for computers
    99.5% (r4.2) and 98.8% (r5.2) of new computers are someone else's personal PC, under an 80% ownership rule. Every top-K method recalls 8% or less of them; team and department unions recall 82 to 97% but grant 70 to 256 PCs each. Test: least_privilege_full.py.
    
  
    
  
    How it was tuned without labelsthe correction
    
    
    Settings were picked on the last training month, by how many of each person's next-month new sites the top 20 caught. No insider labels and no test data are involved. The build plan fixes rank 64, alpha 0.5 and regularization 1.0 on log(1 + active days), using the numpy version in recommender_als_simple.py. Spark's ALS scales regularization differently, so if the team uses it, it must be re-tuned the same way. A later, wider search found rank 128, alpha 0.1, regularization 10 a little better (0.232 and 0.202); both settings beat popularity. Tests: recommender_als_simple.py, least_privilege_full.py, recommender-as-detector reruns.
    
  
    
  
    Why not RMSE?a common question
    
    
    RMSE (root mean squared error) measures how far predicted numbers are from true numbers. An implicit-feedback recommender predicts preferences, not visit counts, so it is judged by ranking [13]: of the sites a person actually visits for the first time next, how many were in its top 20 (recall@20)? That is the number that matters for pre-approval.
    
  
    
  
    How ALS actually learns a "taste"for the curious
    
    
    Picture the person-by-site table above with thousands of rows. ALS finds, for every person, a list of 64 numbers (their taste vector), and for every site 64 numbers (its profile), so that a person's vector times a site's profile approximates how much they use it. It alternates: fix the sites and solve for people, then fix the people and solve for sites, until it settles. That's the "alternating least squares".
    
    
    "Implicit feedback" means we have no ratings, only usage, so heavier use counts as more confidence [13]. We feed log(1 + active days), so a site someone opens every day doesn't drown out everything else.
    
  
    
  
    Prior workfor the pitch
    
    
    Scoring access by how unexpected it is under a collaborative-filtering model has been used against hospital-record snooping (Menon and colleagues, 2014) [9], with related community-based work in CADS [10], and Google's insider-detection system Facade [11] builds on who-accessed-what. On CERT, our controlled tests found it added nothing as a detector, so we use it the other way round: to grant what people will need, which shrinks what anyone can misuse.

    12 · Tried and dropped
    
  
    Two good-sounding ideas the tests ruled out
    
  
    Both are well known and both were tested on the same data with the same rules. Neither is in the build, and here is why, in numbers.
    
  
    
    
    dropped · step 20a
    Analyst feedback (active anomaly discovery)
    After each batch of reviews, the analyst's verdicts re-weight the detector families so the next batch favors what was confirmed (Das et al., ICDM 2016 [27]). Result: 57 vs 55 insiders found in 100 reviews on r4.2, and 42 vs 43 on r5.2. Across 40 subsamples the gain is +0.2 ± 0.9 and +0.1 ± 1.0 insiders, which is noise. It does generalize: with job-hunters hidden it places 22 of 30 in the top 100, against 19 for the frozen fusion and 4 to 7 for trained combiners. But at 100 reviews it adds nothing over the frozen ranking. Test: the analyst-feedback experiment.
    
    
    dropped · steps 4, 15, 20b
    The recommender as a detector
    Items tried: site, site × hour, computer × hour, email recipients, sequences, peer groups. None beat popularity. Site × hour popularity raises r5.2 web novelty from 11.4 to 26.0 expected hits in the top 70, but popularity alone gets that gain; collaborative-filtering variants reach 22 to 26 (user-kNN ties popularity at 26.0). After label-free tuning, detection stayed flat or got worse (3.0 and 4.0 hits@70 on site) and added nothing in a logistic combiner (AP change -0.000 and -0.001, against seed noise of ±0.005). Test: recommender-as-detector reruns.
    
  
    
  
    What we kept from them. The recommender found its real job in least privilege (section 10). The feedback idea's lesson, that label-free methods generalize to unseen attacks, matches step 7 and is why the build uses no labels.

    Day-by-day replay: own-history weekly percentile in the buildearly warning
    How it works. Every day, for each person, the last 7 days of nine counts (logons, USB connects, file copies and web visits, each also after hours, plus distinct computers) are compared with each of the person's training weeks. A count is unusual when it beats every one of them. The number of unusual counts feeds a running score that halves every 7 days, and the 20 highest scores form that day's list.
    Where it came from. Weekly own-history percentiles from Le & Zincir-Heywood 2021 [5], turned into a day-by-day replay with no look-ahead because the v3 study could not say whether we would catch anyone in time.
    What our tests showed. 59 of 70 (r4.2) and 80 of 99 (r5.2) insiders listed before their last malicious action; about half of each attack still to come (54%, 48%); about 5 first-time names a week. Two-day sysadmin sabotage mostly missed (4 of 10, 2 of 10). Test: early_warning_full.py, same counts from early_warning_simple.py.

    Decision. Build: the headline (role R2). Chosen after seeing results; the pre-registered fusion also beat the baseline.
    
    
    Evidence rule C1: new to this person, rarest first in the buildcase files
    How it works. A person's activity becomes items (a computer logged into on a day, a site visited on a day, a USB day, an after-hours day). Items the person never used in training come first; within each group, the item fewest staff used comes first. The top items form the evidence pack.
    Where it came from. First-seen novelty (Garchery & Granitzer [8]) and rarity filtering (Google Facade [11]), compared head to head in our pre-registered evidence-ranking experiment.
    What our tests showed. For insiders flagged in time, using evidence up to the flag day: hit@3 0.64 (r4.2) and 0.85 (r5.2), against 0.49 and 0.51 for global rarity.     Reply check and evidence strength in the buildcase files
    How it works. Granite returns JSON with exactly two keys, a label from a fixed list of five and the IDs of the supporting facts; it writes no free text. Code accepts the reply only if the keys, the label and every ID are valid (and the label is "weak evidence" when the evidence is weak). Anything else is discarded and a deterministic fallback labelled "needs review" or "weak evidence" is shown, so it fails closed. Code renders every fact sentence from the pack, always shows the top 3 ranked items and marks the ones Granite cited. Each case file also states its evidence strength.
    Where it came from. Audit-LLM [20] (LLM explanations drift from the facts) and OWASP LLM01 [21] (log text can carry injected instructions).
    What our tests showed. A guard, not a scored detector. The self-test (case_file.py) rejects 12 bad replies without crashing, including "Alice Smith stole customer records" and "They copied five hundred customer files" sent as notes. On evidence strength: 80 to 92% of flagged normal staff have no item above the median insider's top C1 score, but neither do 44 to 56% of insiders flagged in time, so it is context, never a reason to dismiss. Test: evidence_ranking.py.

    How it works. For each week and count, the share of the person's training weeks that were lower; a week where counts beat everything before is a spike.
    Where it came from. Le & Zincir-Heywood 2021 [5].
    What our tests showed. r4.2: 39.4 found in the top 70, AP 0.666, P@20 1.00. r5.2: AP 0.477, P@20 1.00. Test: v3 study.
    Before → after. Step 10: R2 38 / 0.728 → 39.4 / 0.666 found / AP (+1.4 found).
    Decision. Became the core of the day-by-day replay.
    
    
    Family-first Stouffer fusion referencefusion
    How it works. Turns each detector's rank into a p-value, keeps the best per family, then adds the families' evidence on a z-scale.
    Where it came from. Outlier-ensemble methods [19].
    What our tests showed. r4.2: 45 found, AP 0.784. Frozen for r5.2: 38, AP 0.478. At 100 reviews: 55 vs 44 (r4.2), 43 vs 39 (r5.2). With job-hunters hidden, 19 of 30 in its top 100. Tests: v3 study; Codex review round 4.
    Before → after. Steps 11, 12, 14: R2 38 → 45 (r4.2); 36 → 38 (r5.2); at 100 reviews 44 / 39 → 55 / 43.
    Decision. Reference: the v3 default ranking; the build uses the daily list.
    
    
    Weekly persistence on unusual computers referenceout-of-role
    How it works. Counts the weeks someone logs into computers they did not use before, discounted for people whose training already showed roaming.
    Where it came from. Our r5.2 findings and the outside reviewer's controlled test.
    What our tests showed. 27 of 30 snoopers in its top 100 on r5.2, whether novelty is counted by ownership, naively or with the recommender (27 to 30 across versions). Test: v3 study.
    Before → after. Step 13: naive new computers 10.9 → 27 of 30 snoopers (+16).
    Decision. Reference: snoopers are covered by the daily list (25 of 30 in time on r5.2).
    
    
    Weekly LOF referenceslow drift
    How it works. Asks whether a person's week sits in a much emptier neighborhood than its neighbors do.
    Where it came from. Weekly granularity from Le & Zincir-Heywood [5].
    What our tests showed. 12 to 13 of 30 job-hunters in its top 100 on r4.2. Test: v3 study.
    Before → after. Step 8: R2 5 → 12 to 13 of 30 job-hunters (+7 to +8).
    Decision. Reference: the daily list flags 30 of 30 r4.2 job-hunters in time (median 11 days).
    
    
    Rare sites referencenovelty
    How it works. Counts visits to sites few staff visited in training.
    Where it came from. Google Facade: filtering accesses common to many users [11].
    What our tests showed. 41 found on r4.2 but 8.1 on r5.2 as a detector. As evidence, global rarity alone: hit@3 0.49 and 0.51 at flag time. Tests: v3 study; evidence_ranking.py.
    Before → after. Step 4: R2 38 → 41 found (r4.2); does not transfer (r5.2: 8.1).
    Decision. Lives on inside C1 as the second key, "rarest first".
    
    
    Naive novelty: new computers and sites referencenovelty
    How it works. Counts the distinct computers or websites a person never used during training.
    Where it came from. First-seen novelty, as in Garchery & Granitzer [8].
    What our tests showed. Added to behaviour + rare: 39 → 41 found (r4.2). As evidence alone at flag time: hit@3 0.54 and 0.28. Tests: v3 study; evidence_ranking.py.
    Before → after. Step 5: behaviour + rare 39 → 41 (+2).
    Decision. Lives on inside C1 as the first key, "new to this person".
    
    
    Label-free robust sum referencefusion
    How it works. Adds up each detector's robust z-score, with no labels.
    Where it came from. Common practice.
    What our tests showed. With job-hunters hidden from training: 16 of 30 in the top 100, against 4 to 7 for trained models. Test: v3 study.
    Before → after. Step 7: trained 4 to 7 → label-free 16 of 30 (+9 to +12).
    Decision. Its lesson (no labels) shapes the build; the sum itself is not used.
    
    
    Recommender surprise as a detector (v3 setting) droppedrecommender
    How it works. ALS with rank 16, alpha 10, regularization 0.1; a new site's surprise is 1 minus its predicted affinity.
    Where it came from. Our abstract; CS 6350 Lab 3 [24]; precedent in Menon 2014 [9].
    What our tests showed. 5 found on r4.2 (AUC 0.71). At its own job, mean percentile rank 35.9 vs 35.5 for popularity. Test: v3 study.
    Before → after. Step 4: R2 38 → 5 (-33). Step 15: popularity 35.5 → 35.9 MPR (worse by 0.4).
    Decision. Dropped as a detector; superseded by the tuned model's real job (step 16).
    
    
    Richer recommender items as detectors droppedrecommender
    How it works. Recommenders over site, site × hour, computer × hour, email recipients, sequences and peer groups.
    Where it came from. Our abstract's idea, extended after the v3 study.
    What our tests showed. No gain over popularity. Site × hour popularity raises r5.2 web novelty from 11.4 to 26.0 expected hits in the top 70; CF variants reach 22 to 26 (user-kNN ties popularity at 26.0). Tuned, detection stays flat or worse (3.0 and 4.0 hits@70 on site), and it adds nothing in a logistic combiner (AP change -0.000 and -0.001; seed noise ±0.005). Test: recommender-as-detector reruns.
    Before → after. Step 20b: site rarity 11.4 → site × hour popularity 26.0; CF variants 22 to 26 (+14.6 from context; CF adds nothing).
    Decision. Dropped.
    
    
    Site peer-of-item droppedrecommender
    How it works. For a new site, how similar you are to the most similar person who visited it before.
    Where it came from. Microsoft (Modell et al.) [12]; Google Facade [11].
    What our tests showed. 17 found on r4.2, AUC 0.84. Test: v3 study.
    Before → after. Step 9: ALS site surprise 5 → 17 (+12).
    Decision. Not in the build: never reached the baseline's 38.
    
    
    Recommender as explanation evidence (agreement filter) droppedcase files
    How it works. Cite an item only if it is both rare and surprising to the recommender.
    Where it came from. A coordinator request in the evidence experiment.
    What our tests showed. Collapses to C1 on r4.2 and on the top-100 view; trails C1 on r5.2 across all insiders (0.891 vs 0.922). Test: evidence_ranking.py.
    Decision. Dropped; the optional mixed rule is the better use of the recommender.
    
    
    Analyst feedback re-weighting (AAD) droppedfeedback
    How it works. After each batch of reviews, the analyst's verdicts re-weight the detector families so the next batch favors what was confirmed.
    Where it came from. Das et al., ICDM 2016 [27].
    What our tests showed. 57 vs 55 insiders found in 100 reviews (r4.2), 42 vs 43 (r5.2). Across 40 subsamples: +0.2 ± 0.9 and +0.1 ± 1.0. With job-hunters hidden: 22 of 30, against 19 for frozen fusion and 4 to 7 for trained combiners. Test: the analyst-feedback experiment.
    Before → after. Step 20a: frozen Stouffer 55 / 43 → 57 / 42 (+2 / -1; noise +0.2 ± 0.9).
    Decision. Dropped: the gain is noise.
    
    
    Trained combiner (logistic regression) droppedfusion
    How it works. Learns how much to trust each detector from labelled cases; each person is scored by a model that never saw them.
    Where it came from. Standard stacking; supervised setups in Le et al. [6].
    What our tests showed. 45 found with all signals and 49 with ALS added (r4.2). With job-hunters hidden from training: 5 to 7 of 30. Test: v3 study.
    Before → after. Step 5: behaviour + rare 39 → 45 with ALS. Step 6: all signals 45 → 49 (+4).
    Decision. Dropped: needs labels and fails on unseen attacks.
    
    
    Gradient boosting combiner droppedfusion
    How it works. Many small decision trees, each fixing the previous ones' mistakes.
    Where it came from. Standard machine learning.
    What our tests showed. 60 of 70 found, but only 4 of 30 hidden job-hunters. Test: v3 study.
    Before → after. Step 6: LR 45 → 60 (+15). Step 7: 4 of 30 hidden job-hunters.
    Decision. Dropped: too good to be true, and it doesn't generalize.
    
    
    Specialist queues droppedfusion
    How it works. Each detector fills its own review list, and each alert carries that detector's reason.
    Where it came from. Splunk risk-based alerting [16], Exabeam [17], Sentinel [14].
    What our tests showed. At 100 reviews: 42 vs 55 for fusion on r4.2, 53 vs 43 on r5.2. Test: v3 study.
    Before → after. Step 14: R2 44 / 39 → four queues 42 / 53.
    Decision. Dropped: won only where snoopers needed a list of their own.
    
    
    Other checks from the earlier study droppedearlier study
    How it works. Hand rule (after-hours USB days), daily z-score baseline, 7- and 56-day windows, team peer comparison, Isolation Forest, autoencoder, popularity-lift correction, 28-day site-visit lift, rank sums, threshold-sized queues and an industry-style family maximum.
    Where it came from. Common practice, the reviewer's critique, and published CERT studies [5][7].
    What our tests showed. Each was scored in the v3 study [0]. None is in the build, and their numbers are not in the verified set, so none are quoted here.
    Decision. Not in the build.

    15 · Can we trust it?
    
  
    The tests that keep us honest
    
  
    It's easy to fool yourself with machine learning. These are the checks behind every number on this page, and the places where they changed what we claim.
    
  
    
    
    no look-ahead
    The replay uses only days up to "today". Training ends before the cutoff (r4.2: 2010-06-01; r5.2: 2010-04-01) with the cutoff week dropped; labels are used only for grading; departures are never read. The independent validator found no look-ahead and no label leakage in any scoring code.
    
    
    a second dataset
    Settings were frozen on r4.2, then r5.2 ran unchanged: 80 of 99 in time. A one-month-later cutoff also left the early-warning headline intact.
    
    
    independent code
    Each number was reproduced by separately written code: early_warning_simple.py gives the same counts as early_warning_full.py, and recommender_als_simple.py checks the recommender.
    
    
    strong comparisons
    Every early-warning result is shown against chance (about 3), a random score (10.3, 11.4), the 28-day baseline under our running rule (33, 28) and, above all, the same baseline on the same 20-name list (59, 40).
    
    
    unseen attacks
    In the earlier study, trained models caught only 4 to 7 of 30 hidden job-hunters, against 16 for a label-free sum (step 7). That is why the build uses no labels.
    
    
    outside review
    An independent validator replaced our 98% evidence figure with 64% / 85% (evidence up to the flag day). Codex's round 4 review added the fair baseline, the leaker caveat and the 28% / 42% grant use, and asked us to state the pivot.
    
  
    
  
    Disclosures we make up frontfrom the validation
    
    
      
      
    • The early-warning percentile method was chosen after seeing results. The pre-registered fusion flagged 60 of 70 and 54 of 99 (54.5%) in time.
      • 
      
    • Each train cutoff was set to precede every attack, a choice that used the labels.
      • 
      
    • least_privilege_full.py trains through the day before the cutoff rather than dropping the cutoff week. This is not leakage, because every attack starts after the cutoff.
      • 
      
    • Requests per person-month include staff who have since left in the denominator. Both policies share it, so the -21% / -19% comparison holds.
      • 
      
    • The mixed evidence rule (top 2 C1 items plus 1 recommender item) was designed after the fact and is optional.
      • 
    
    
  
    
  
    What we still can't claimsay it in the pitch
    
    
      
      
    • A replay, not a production system. Say "flags insiders while they are still active", not "real-time".
      • 
      
    • On r4.2 we only tie the fair baseline (59 vs 59); the doubling is on r5.2 (80 vs 40).
      • 
      
    • Leakers' 0-day delay is flattered by being already on the list: only 7 of 30 and 15 of 29 were newly flagged, and 25 of 30 and 24 of 29 show unlabelled after-hours USB activity the week before their labelled start.
      • 
      
    • Two-day sysadmin sabotage is mostly missed (4 of 10, 2 of 10).
      • 
      
    • The advisor suggests; it does not decide. Only 28% and 42% of its extra grants were later used.
      • 
      
    • The data is synthetic, and insiders make up 5 to 7% of staff, which is unrealistically high.

    16 · Judges and our edge
    
  
    How we score on each judging criterion
    
  
    Four criteria, 10 points each, judged by IBM experts from a 3-minute video [25]. Here's our case for each, and the evidence we'll show.
    
  
    The pitch line (from the build plan): "In a day-by-day replay of 2,000 employees' logs, OutOfRole put 80 of 99 insiders on a 20-name daily review list while their attacks were under way, twice as many as a standard 28-day baseline on the same 20-name list, and on average about half of each one's malicious actions came after the flag. Granite, on the same server, labels each case and picks the supporting evidence, which code writes into a checked case file. A recommender suggests which access to pre-approve, so reviewers see fewer routine requests."
    
  
    State the pivot openly. Our abstract promised recommender-based detection. Tests showed that own-history baselines detect better (steps 4, 15 and 20b), so the recommender moved to access advice, where it beats popularity (step 16). Judges reward a team that tested its own idea and changed course.
    
  
    
    
    10pts
    Value to the community and theme fit
    Insiders are flagged while they are still active, before about half of their malicious actions on average, and routine access requests shrink. Records in banks, hospitals and agencies are what is at stake (61,104 benefit accounts in Texas; 9.7 million people at Desjardins), and the data never leaves the platform: "Innovation without exposure", literally.
    
      
    Evidence and risks
    Show: the replay with a leaker joining the list, and the access panel. Risk: sounding like surveillance. Answer: pseudonyms (U-017); about 5 first-time names a week for a human to review; every case file cites checked evidence and states its strength; the advisor only suggests pre-approvals.
    
    
    10pts
    Novelty and originality
    Most CERT studies rank people after the fact; we measure whether they would be caught in time, against a fair baseline. Granite writes no free text in the case files: it picks a label and fact IDs, and code writes every fact. And the recommender, which failed as a detector, found a real job in least privilege.
    
      
    Evidence and risks
    Show: the step ladder (section 11), including the dropped ideas. Risk: "your original idea didn't work". Answer: we tested it properly, said so, and found where it does help (1.45x popularity on next-site recall).
    
    
    10pts
    Technical design and use of LinuxONE
    Spark on s390x (no pyarrow there) turns 15 to 37 GB of logs into three tables; a numpy replay and ALS; Granite through llama.cpp with a big-endian model file; everything in the event's Docker container.
    
      
    Evidence and risks
    Show: uname -m printing s390x, docker ps, the server prompt, and a case file where the reply check accepts Granite's label and fact IDs and code marks the cited facts. Both required IBM labs completed. Risk: setup eats the day. Answer: the cut rules in section 17.
    
    
    10pts
    Execution and pitch
    A 3-minute demo: the replay plays, a case file appears with Granite's cited facts marked, the access panel blocks wikileaks.org. Each teammate's role is named.
    
      
    Evidence and risks
    Three numbers: 80 of 99 in time (twice the fair baseline's 40); 85% of in-time r5.2 insiders with real evidence in the top 3 (64% on r4.2); 21% vs 14.5% next-site recall. Say openly: the r4.2 tie, the missed sysadmins, synthetic data, and that the percentile method was picked after seeing results.
    
  
    
  
    Bonus prize, AI Innovation Excellence: judged on originality and technical sophistication. Our case: an early-warning replay with honest controls, an LLM limited to a label and fact IDs, with every fact written by code and any other reply rejected, and a recommender moved to the job it is actually good at.

    Not just "who is suspicious" but "would we have caught them while it mattered", against chance, a random list and a fair baseline.

    Each flag has a case file: Granite picks a label and evidence IDs, code writes every fact, and any other reply falls back to a safe default.
