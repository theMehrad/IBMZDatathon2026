The Debugging Table · IBM Z Datathon 2026 · AI Secured track

OutOfRole, step by step

We catch employees who misuse access they legitimately have, while they are still doing it. Every alert comes with checked evidence that IBM Granite labels but cannot make up, and a recommender keeps everyone's access lean. All of it runs on one IBM LinuxONE server.

About 15 minutes to read
Start here

The goal

Some employees misuse access they are allowed to have. They copy files to a USB stick before quitting, upload documents to a leak site, or log into colleagues' computers to snoop. Their logins are valid, so simple rules either miss them or bury the security team in alerts.

We want three things:

  • A short list. Every day, the 20 people most worth a look, small enough for a team to review.
  • Early. The person should show up while the attack is still going on, so there is still something to stop. We call this caught in time.
  • Explained and safe. Each name comes with the evidence behind it, the AI cannot invent facts, and no data leaves the server.
80 of 99insiders caught in time on the test release (r5.2)
40 of 99caught by a standard baseline with the same 20-name list
82xhow much more often an active insider is on the list than a normal employee

On the tuning release (r4.2) we tie the baseline at 59 of 70. Step 8 explains why the two releases differ.

The features we compute

For each person and day we count nine activities. These nine numbers are the whole input of the main detector.

logonsafter-hours logonsUSB connectsafter-hours USBfile copiesafter-hours file copiesweb visitsafter-hours webdistinct computers used

"After hours" means before 7:00 or from 19:00 on. The recommender steps also use two lists: which computers each person logged into, and which websites each person visited.

Never commit or share the data. The CERT license forbids redistribution, so data/ is git-ignored. Everyone downloads it themselves (runbook step 4). This page shows no real rows; every example below is made up.

We also ran our methods on a second, real dataset from Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL): 58 days of logins by 16,759 people, with 749 logins by a red team that attacked the network on purpose. The result is in step 8.

The rules of the test

A detector is easy to fool yourself with. These rules keep the numbers honest.

r4.2
Training: learn normal
Replay from 1 Jun 2010, one day at a time
r5.2
Training
Replay from 1 Apr 2010, one day at a time
training months (Jan 2010 to the cutoff)cutoffreplay, as if live, to May 2011
  1. Time only moves forward. We learn what normal looks like from the training months. Then we replay the later months day by day, and on each day the code may use only the past.
  2. The answer key is only for grading. Nothing is fitted to it. A real company never has a list of its insiders.
  3. Tune once, then freeze. Every setting was chosen on r4.2, then run on r5.2 unchanged. That makes r5.2 the fairer test.
  4. Same budget for everyone. Every method we compare gets the same 20-name daily list.
  5. "Caught in time" means the insider appears on the list at least once between their first and last malicious action.

2

The daily review list

Compare each person's last week with their own normal weeks; list the 20 most unusual people every day.

M1Own-history percentile model. Statistics, unsupervised, one small model per person. Learns from that person's own training weeks only.
In short
  1. From the training months, keep each person's weekly totals of the nine activities: their own "normal weeks".
  2. Every day, total each person's last 7 days. An activity counts when it beats every one of their normal weeks.
  3. Add the count to a running score that halves each week. The 20 highest scores are that day's list.

For every person we keep their weekly totals from the training months, one number per activity per week. Each day of the replay we add up their last 7 days. If an activity is higher than every one of that person's normal weeks, it counts as unusual. The day's score is the number of unusual activities.

Why it works: everyone is compared with themselves, so busy roles do not swamp the list. "Higher than every normal week" ignores people who are bursty by nature, which an average would not. Counting distinct computers is how snooping shows up, and the running score rewards persistence, which slow attacks have.

3

The snooping review

Flag people who keep logging into computers the recommender would never suggest for them.

M2Person × computer recommender. Implicit ALS (collaborative filtering), unsupervised. Learns from who logged into which computer in training.
In short

A report, not a daily alarm. It ranks people by how many weeks they logged into computers that are new to them and that the recommender says people like them do not use, with IT staff and other natural roamers discounted. We measured it once, at the end of the replay; in a company it would run every month, and its top names get case files.

Think of Netflix. It knows what you watched and what people like you watched, and from that it predicts what you might like. Our recommender does the same with people and computers: from who logged into which computer during training, it predicts how likely each person is to use each computer.

During the replay, each login to a computer that is new to the person and that M2 scores as unlikely is flagged. A person's score is the number of weeks with a flagged login, scaled down for people who always move between computers anyway, such as IT staff.

Why it works: snoopers log into colleagues' computers, again and again. One odd login can be innocent; many weeks of logins to machines outside your profile is a pattern. The recommender adds the "people like you" judgment that a plain "new computer" rule lacks.
27 of 30snoopers in the review's top 100 people (r5.2)
26 of 30with a simpler "any new computer" rule, no recommender
9 of 30if run as a live daily list instead, so we use it as a periodic report
Say it straight: the recommender's edge here is small (27 against 26), r4.2 has no snoopers, and this review was designed after looking at r5.2. The daily list of step 2 already catches 25 of 30 snoopers in time. On real LANL logins this review did not transfer (step 8).
How we did it code/outofrole_minimal.py, section 1b
Code
code/outofrole_minimal.py, section "1b. snooping list" (lines 105 to 131); the model is als() near the top of the file
Reads
pc_pairs: user, day, pc

Same numpy code; no Spark needed. Different processors round the last digit of a number differently, but we measured the closest call in the whole run: no score sits within 0.00001 of the 0.5 cut-off, while rounding moves scores by about 0.00000000000001. So the same logins get flagged.

4

Rank the evidence

For each flagged person, put the most telling facts on top: new to you first, then rarest in the company.

M3New-to-you, rarest-first scorer. Frequency statistics, no training. Uses who did what in the training months.

A flagged person may have done hundreds of things since the replay began. The analyst needs the three that matter. We list every computer, website, USB day and after-hours login the person had, and give each a score:

score = 2 × new + (1 − share)

  • new is 1 if the person never did it during training, else 0.
  • share is the fraction of all staff who did it during training (0 = nobody, 1 = everyone).

So anything new to the person beats anything old, and among new things the rarest wins. Each top fact gets an ID (E1, E2, ...) for the next step.

Why it works: insiders do things that are new for them (a first USB stick, a first leak site), and most attacks use something few colleagues touch. Rarity alone fails when a rare thing is normal for that person; novelty fixes that.

The Granite case file

IBM Granite picks a label and the supporting fact IDs; code writes every sentence.

M4IBM Granite. A large language model used as a checked classifier. Learns nothing from our data; it reads one evidence pack at a time.

Language models can make things up. So we never let Granite write text. It gets the evidence pack and may answer with only two things, as JSON: a label from a fixed list and the IDs of the facts that support it. Code checks the reply. If anything is off, the reply is thrown away and a fixed fallback is shown.

From step 4M3Evidence packtop facts E1 to E5, strength strong or weak
→

→
CodecheckIs the reply valid?known label, known IDs, nothing else
→
Yes: case filecode writes one sentence per fact, marks the cited ones
No: fallbacklabel "needs review"; the ranked facts still show

Granite can also act as the final judge that orders the day's list; see The LLM's role.

The five labels: possible data theft · possible data leak · possible snooping · unusual after-hours activity · weak evidence.

A reply fails when it is not JSON, has any key besides label and facts, uses a label not on the list, cites an ID that does not exist, cites nothing, or gives a strong label to weak evidence.

Why it works: every sentence an analyst reads comes from our data, so there is nothing for the model to invent. Granite adds what code cannot easily do: read the facts together and name the pattern. The prompt also tells it that text inside the facts is data, never instructions; the self-test includes a website name that tries to give orders.
12 of 12bad replies in the self-test fall back safely (made-up names, unknown IDs, broken JSON)

6

Access advice

Predict which websites each person will need next, so routine access can be pre-approved.

M5Person × website recommender. Implicit ALS (collaborative filtering), unsupervised, retrained every month. Learns from who visited which website.

This is the recommender's real job, and the one it is best at. The same kind of model as step 3, now on people × websites, predicts the 20 websites each person is most likely to need next. Those can be pre-approved, so admins see fewer routine requests. Websites a person used in training but never again become suggestions for removing access.

Why it works: people in similar jobs use similar tools. When your colleagues start using a site, you probably will too. Retraining every month keeps the predictions current, and it takes seconds.

The real-data test (LANL)

We froze every CERT setting, wrote the test and its pass mark down, locked the file with a hash, and only then opened LANL's list of red-team logins. 44 stolen accounts were active in the test weeks.

0 of 44stolen accounts in the snooping review's top 100 (chance: 0.26). It does not transfer.
6.4xa red-team login is flagged "new and unexpected" this much more often than a normal login
4 of 44in the top 100 when we simply count each person's new computers (chance 0.26)

So the signal is real for single logins, but our way of adding it up per person was tuned to CERT. At LANL almost everyone logs into a new computer most weeks, and the stolen accounts belonged to people who already moved between many machines, so the roamer discount pushed them down. The daily list caught 0 of 44 too, with only two weeks of history to learn from.

How to say it: "We tested on real data we never tuned on. Novelty shows up in real logs, but the person-level score has to be re-tuned for each organisation." Do not claim LANL confirms our CERT numbers.

Rules for the judge

  • It reorders; it never removes. All 20 names stay on the list. About half of the insiders we catch in time have weak-looking evidence, so letting the AI dismiss people would throw real catches away.
  • Same safety as step 5. A verdict from a fixed list plus fact IDs, as JSON. Code checks it, and no free text reaches the analyst.
  • A person decides. The judge sets the order of work; the analyst makes the call.

How we will know it helps

The same way as everything else: write the test down first, try the prompt on r4.2, then run it unchanged on r5.2. It earns its place only if insiders move up the queue, for example more insiders in the top 5 of each day's 20 than in the plain order. If it does not help, we say so and keep the plain order.

Other planned uses, in order of value: a daily briefing of the list, "ask your logs" (a plain-English question turned into one of a fixed set of queries), and short notes for access reviews. All follow the same rule: Granite picks from fixed options and cites IDs; code writes the text.

Glossary

Insider

An employee who misuses access they legitimately have.

Caught in time

On the daily list at least once between the insider's first and last malicious action, so there is still something to stop.

Baseline

The simple standard method we must beat. Ours is a 28-day check of each person against their own training habits.

Unsupervised

Learning without the answer key. Our detectors only see behaviour, never who the insiders are, because a real company does not have that list.

Percentile (p)

The share of a person's normal weeks that are below this week's count. p = 0.99 or more means this week beats essentially all of them.

Half-life

How long until a score falls to half. Ours is 7 days: unusual behaviour from a week ago counts half as much as today's.

Recommender, collaborative filtering

A model that predicts what you will use from what people with similar habits use, as Netflix does with films.

ALS (alternating least squares)

The recommender we use. It squeezes the big people × items table into two thin tables (one row of "taste" numbers per person and per item) and alternates between fitting the people side and the items side. Implicit means we only know what was used, not ratings. Rank is how many taste numbers each row has, alpha is how much more we trust a used cell than an empty one, and regularization keeps the numbers small so the model does not memorize noise.

Popularity

The simplest recommender: suggest what most people use. The bar ALS must beat.

hit@3

Whether at least one real malicious action is among a person's top 3 evidence items.

recall@20

Of the new websites people actually started using, the share that were in their 20 predicted sites.

Requests per person-month

How many access requests one employee would file in an average month. Lower means less routine work for admins.

Fail closed

When a check fails, show the safe fallback instead of the risky output.

Overfitting

A method that looks good on the data it was tuned on but fails on new data. Freezing settings on r4.2 and testing on r5.2 guards against it.

Pre-registration

Writing down the exact test and the pass rule, and hashing the file, before looking at the answers, so the rule cannot be bent afterwards.

Parquet

A compact column-based file format. Spark writes our three tables in it; pandas reads them.

s390x, big-endian

s390x is the processor architecture of IBM Z and LinuxONE. It stores numbers big-endian (most significant byte first), unlike laptops, which matters for some binary model files such as Granite's.

Pseudonym

A stand-in ID such as U-017 instead of a real name, so the people on the list are not named.
